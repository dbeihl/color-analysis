# Do twelve seasons describe real kinds of human colouring?

> **Framing note (2026-09-12):** This bounded, breadth-first search was completed 2026-09-12. It is an investigation, not a systematic review; a bounded search cannot prove that no paper exists. It is a point-in-time record, not a maintained literature review.

**Research report**<br>
**Date:** 2026-09-12<br>
**Scope:** A bounded, breadth-first review of the provenance, separability, and predictive evidence for seasonal personal-colour systems. This report is an investigation, not a systematic review or a product redesign.

## Executive answer

**This search found no published empirical support for twelve distinct categories of whole-person human colouring, and no validation that assigning a person to one of twelve seasons predicts which colours suit them.** The twelve are a practitioner convention: an expansion of earlier two-, four-, and eight-part systems whose divisions were asserted or taught, not recovered from measurements of people.

That negative answer is publishable and should be stated without qualification that implies missing evidence is expected to rescue the system. Measured studies do find structure in individual pigmentation traits: skin, hair, and iris measurements vary by ancestry and correlate weakly; quantitative iris colour can look bimodal or admit three- or four-cluster solutions in particular cohorts. Those findings do **not** recover twelve joint skin–hair–iris types. Large studies instead describe much of the variation as continuous.[^1][^2][^3]

There is limited evidence that visible skin characteristics affect third-party judgements of adjacent garment colours. The strongest study located found a repeatable cool-versus-warm garment-hue shift between deliberately selected fair and tanned White faces, but no value effect and an inconsistent saturation effect.[^4] A Thai colour-harmony experiment found effects of undertone and skin lightness, with results that partly contradicted four-season recommendations.[^5] Neither study assigned real people to seasonal categories, and neither supports twelve.

For this product, the defensible current statement is therefore:

> The result is the nearest match among twelve designed palette recipes. The recipes are a widely taught convention, not twelve natural kinds of people, and their ability to predict which colours suit someone has not been validated.

Better iris and hair references would improve input coverage. They cannot validate the number twelve or turn internally separated recipes into observed human groups. That distinction matters because the repository already showed that better-separated references reduced Deep Autumn concentration while increasing low-confidence results: coverage and discriminability are different properties.

## FINDINGS

### 1. Provenance: where the twelve came from

#### Direct answer

The lineage I could verify is an expanding practitioner convention, not an evidential sequence:

| Stage | Traceable source | What changed | Stated or recoverable basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two groups | Robert Dorr's Color Key system, described in Collin's 1986 audit | Blue-key and yellow-key harmony groups | An artist/practitioner colour-harmony system; no measured population study was located.[^6] |
| Four seasons | Suzanne Caygill is credited in practitioner literature with the seasonal analogy; Bernice Kentner published *Color Me a Season* in 1978; Carole Jackson popularised four seasons in *Color Me Beautiful* in 1980 | Warm/cool ideas became Spring, Summer, Autumn, and Winter appearance-and-palette types | Descriptive analogy to nature, client observation, and draping practice. The books report no sampled population, measurement protocol, cluster analysis, or predictive trial.[^6][^7][^8][^9] |
| Eight groups | Fujii and von Alten, *Color with Style* (1992), as reported by the later quantitative Korean paper | Added high/low skin–hair contrast within warm/light and cool/dark divisions | A practitioner classification rule, not a discovered group count.[^10][^11] |
| Twelve palettes | Mary Spillane and Christine Sherlock, *Color Me Beautiful's Looking Your Best* (1995), the earliest twelve-palette publication independently verified in this search | Expanded the four seasons into 12 palettes, including Light, Soft, Clear/Bright, Deep, Warm, and Cool variants | Descriptive appearance rules plus visual comparisons. The Light Summer page, for example, describes fair hair, delicate eyes, light skin, then asks the reader to compare a warm Light Spring palette with a cool Light Summer palette. No participant sample, measured boundaries, or validation statistics are reported in the material inspected.[^12] |

Carole Jackson's 1980 book explicitly sells the four seasons as descriptions of people and their “best colors”; its supporting material gives a practitioner biography, not an empirical study.[^9] Spillane and Sherlock's publisher record explicitly says the system was “expanded to 12 color palettes.”[^12] I did **not** establish that Spillane and Sherlock were the first people ever to use every twelve-season label, because proprietary training lineages and some primary books were not fully accessible. I did establish the narrower and product-relevant point: the earliest twelve-palette publication verified here presents a descriptive consultation convention, with no evidential step from four or eight naturally occurring classes to twelve.

Collin's 1986 master's thesis is the strongest contemporary audit of the pre-twelve printed field. She selected 11 mass-market personal-colour books aimed at women, measured their printed recommendations in Munsell notation under standardised viewing conditions, and compared category names, counts, hue, value, chroma, and agreement between prose and colour chips. Systems used between two and eight categories; five used four seasons. Even systems sharing category names had few colour recommendations in common, written and printed recommendations agreed poorly, no system achieved more than 50% overall written-to-chip agreement, and Collin found no discernible theoretical basis for the recommendations.[^6] This is evidence that the convention existed and was internally inconsistent. It is not a study of whether any system suited people.

**Finding:** Twelve is traceable as a practitioner expansion, but this search found no measured human dataset, clustering result, or predictive experiment that justified the expansion to twelve.

### 2. Separability: do people cluster into discrete colouring groups?

#### Direct answer

The published measurements inspected do not support twelve discrete whole-person colouring groups. They support continuous, population-dependent variation in skin, hair, and iris pigmentation, with some trait-specific structure—most notably blue/brown modes in iris colour. No located paper jointly measured skin, hair, and iris, asked the data how many natural groups it contained, replicated that group count, and recovered twelve.

The most relevant evidence is:

- Candille et al. quantitatively measured 470 Europeans recruited in Dublin, Warsaw, Rome, and Porto. Skin melanin index was approximately normal, hair melanin was unimodal and skewed toward lighter values, and an iris colour coordinate was bimodal with blue and brown modes and green/hazel intermediate. Skin, hair, and eye pigmentation correlations of about .40–.47 fell to roughly .13–.18 after accounting for country and sex. The result is trait distributions plus modest shared structure, not joint seasonal classes.[^1]

- Norton et al. measured skin melanin, hair CIELAB, and high-resolution iris CIELAB in a multi-ethnic cohort of 1,450 adults self-identifying as African American, East Asian, European, Hispanic, or South Asian. Their stated conclusion is that quantitative hair and iris measurements reinforce the continuous rather than discrete nature of pigmentation. Population means differ, but population differences are not twelve personal-colour groups.[^2]

- Paparazzo et al. photographed 238 Calabrian participants' irises under D55 coaxial lighting, separated ciliary and pupillary zones, converted RGB measurements to CIELAB, and compared multiple unsupervised algorithms. Their chosen four-cluster result separated blue, intermediate, and two brown groups. However, they intentionally considered solutions of at least three clusters because the forensic IrisPlex system predicts three eye colours; their own discussion notes that a simpler blue/brown distinction may be more defensible because intermediate labels are subjective. This is credible evidence that **iris colour alone** can have useful local cluster structure. It is not evidence for four whole-person seasons, still less twelve.[^3]

- Kim, Oh, and Lee measured cheek, forehead, and chin colour on 993 Korean women aged in their twenties and thirties. The paper's eight types were not discovered clusters. The authors began with a predeclared three-axis system—yellow-blue, value, and saturation—and created eight octants. For 52 expert-labelled cases, they used the experts' class proportions to choose percentile cut points across the 993 measurements. There was no held-out taxonomy validation, and no suitability outcome. The study quantifies a convention but does not show that humans naturally form eight groups.[^10]

- Lee and Park measured facial colour in 136 female university students and used discriminant analysis to reproduce sensory seasonal labels. Because the labels came from the sensory assignment the model was trained to reproduce, the analysis measures label reproducibility, not whether the groups exist independently or predict suitable colours.[^13]

- Deep Armocromia and the 2026 Lumina classifier do not change this conclusion. Deep Armocromia labels face images with expert four-season and twelve-subtype labels; Lumina extracts skin, hair, and eye features and trains supervised models to predict those labels. Its best four-class test accuracy is about .556 with macro-F1 about .537, and Spring recall below .42. These are measurements of how reproducibly a model can imitate convention labels under uncontrolled imagery, not cluster discovery or external validation.[^14][^15]

#### The soft-versus-light question

The literature located does not establish a natural soft/light split by chroma, by value, or by a combination of the two.

Practitioner-derived quantitative systems define both axes in advance. Kim et al.'s model, for example, makes “light” a high-value division and “mute” a low-saturation division, but its eight octants are imposed rather than recovered.[^10] The strongest independent suitability study found a skin-lightness-related hue effect; garment value did not differ between fair and tanned conditions, and a small saturation effect appeared in one experiment but did not replicate in the second.[^4] Sirisayan found skin lightness mattered to harmony judgements and broadly lower-chroma garment samples were preferred, but did not establish person clusters named Light and Soft.[^5]

The repository's own state is therefore not externally vindicated. Its Light and Soft palettes share the identical Munsell chroma ladder, their mean CIELAB chroma differs by less than one, and they are separated in practice by value alone (`docs/palette-derivation.md:96-102`). The underlying axes are explicitly “qualitative design intent” rather than fitted measurements (`docs/palette-derivation.md:71-72`), and the regions are deliberately overlapping rather than claimed natural classes (`docs/palette-derivation.md:94`).

**Finding:** Individual pigmentation traits can show continuous variation and limited trait-specific modes or clusters. The inspected evidence does not establish any replicated number of discrete whole-person colouring groups, and it supplies no empirical soft-versus-light boundary.

### 3. Predictive claim: does a category predict which colours suit someone?

#### Direct answer

No inspected study validates the product's twelve-category predictive claim. The limited third-party judgement evidence supports some narrow relationships between displayed skin and adjacent colour. It neither validates a season assignment nor shows that twelve palettes predict suitability better than simpler continuous features, general colour preferences, or chance.

The relevant studies separate into four levels of evidential strength:

1. **Best positive evidence, but for a narrow continuous contrast rather than seasons.** Perrett and Sprengelmeyer selected six fair and six tanned young White women's faces, then asked 96 UK observers in one experiment and 75 in a replication to adjust garment colour beside the faces. Observers chose relatively cooler clothing hues for fair faces and warmer hues for tanned faces: 77% and 73% of participants respectively placed the tanned condition at higher CIE b*. Garment value did not differ. Saturation differed slightly in the first experiment and not in the second. Generic red/blue preferences were strong, the displays were online and uncalibrated, and the faces were selected endpoints rather than natural clusters. This supports a reproducible skin-dependent hue effect within the tested population; it does not support a seasonal taxonomy.[^4]

2. **Direct harmony ratings, partly inconsistent with the convention.** Sirisayan paired 15 Pantone SkinTone Guide samples with 128 four-season device colours and had 30 Thai observers rate all 1,920 flat pairs on a −5 to +5 harmony scale under a calibrated D65 display setup. Undertone and skin lightness affected ratings, but the authors regrouped the skin samples into seven undertone/lightness groups after inspecting the results. Warm samples were best overall with Autumn, yet seven of the ten highest-rated individual pairs for warm skin used Winter colours; Spring performed negatively for medium/low warm samples. Neutral high-lightness samples accepted all seasons, and a generic harmony model correlated only .53 with ratings. Flat swatches are not faces, but this is useful negative and mixed evidence: third-party judgements do not reproduce the simple four-season prescription reliably.[^5]

3. **A direct controlled test that did not find type-specific recommendations.** Hong and Kim showed 431 South Korean undergraduates digitally manipulated images of one Asian woman representing four predeclared skin/hair value-contrast types, paired with four red shirts and a no-shirt condition. High-chroma, medium-value red was broadly preferred. The effect of red shade did not significantly differ by the four physical types. This directly undercuts the tested type-specific recommendation, while remaining limited to one synthetic face and red garments.[^16]

4. **Older or indirect evidence with major limitations.** Radeloff reported that 276 observers agreed with an expert's recommended versus nonrecommended colours in five of six photographed-model sets, but the accessible abstract did not provide the reciprocal colour controls needed to separate person-colour fit from general preference; later authors identify this confound.[^17] Francis and Evans experimentally varied garment hue, value, and style in employment-evaluation images and concluded that value mattered more than hue and the results did not fully support commercial recommendations. The outcome was employability rather than “suitability,” and the accessible record did not expose the sample size.[^18]

Recommendation-system and computer-vision papers are not predictive validation when their target is the convention itself. Su et al. assume a four-season system and optimise recommendation machinery around it; Withurat et al. build decision-tree rules from an existing personal-colour database; Deep Armocromia and Lumina predict expert season labels.[^14][^15][^19][^20] None uses independent blinded raters' judgements of the same people in controlled garment colours as ground truth.

**Finding:** Human colouring can influence some colour-harmony or attractiveness judgements, but no located publication shows that twelve-season assignment predicts those judgements. The evidence is too narrow, inconsistent, or circular to validate the product claim.

## HYPOTHESES

These are inferences from the findings, not things the sources establish:

1. **A continuous, feature-specific account may fit the measurements better than discrete seasons.** This is suggested by continuous pigmentation distributions and by hue effects associated with measured skin lightness or yellowness. It has not been compared against seasonal systems in a preregistered predictive study.

2. **The product's Light/Soft distinction may currently be a value distinction wearing two labels.** That is true of the generated palettes. It may or may not be true of people's responses; the necessary controlled study was not found.

3. **Some apparent success of seasonal consultation may come from the draping comparison itself rather than the season label.** Repeated side-by-side judgement could be useful even if no stable latent category exists. No inspected study isolated this mechanism.

4. **Expert labels may be teachable and reproducible without being natural kinds or predictively valid.** Supervised classifiers show that label imitation is possible above chance. That does not tell us whether the target labels correspond to clusters in people or cause better garment recommendations.

None of these hypotheses is a proposed replacement taxonomy.

## What each possible answer would mean for the product

| Eventual empirical answer | Product consequence | Cost before making a claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Twelve holds** | The product could retain twelve categories, but its present recipes, thresholds, and fixtures would still need to be mapped to the validated groups rather than assumed equivalent. | Obtain or create a representative measured dataset; define the twelve from data; show out-of-sample assignment reliability; validate palette suitability with independent blinded raters; replace implementation-derived fixtures with ground truth; recalibrate confidence. This is a substantial research and model rebuild, not a copy edit. |
| **Fewer groups hold** | Collapse the public taxonomy to the supported count. Do not preserve twelve by subdividing groups without evidence. | Migrate labels, palette pools, adjacency, output copy, tests, and saved results; validate the smaller taxonomy and its suitability predictions independently. This is a breaking domain-model change but smaller than maintaining false precision. |
| **No discrete groups hold** | Do not present a season as a discovered property of a person. If the twelve recipes remain, describe the result as proximity to designed palettes or as a convention used for comparison. If natural classification is essential to the promise, remove the categorical claim. | Rewrite the claim and confidence language, stop treating implementation outputs as ground truth, and validate any retained comparison experience as an experience rather than a diagnosis. This does not imply that colour comparison is useless; it means group membership is unsupported. |

**The current evidence falls in the third row for the question asked:** no discrete whole-person grouping has been established. That does not prove that every possible grouping study will fail, and it does not erase the narrow skin-dependent hue effects found in controlled work. It means the product has no evidential basis today for saying that people come in twelve types.

## What the repository itself establishes—and does not

The repository is admirably explicit about the boundary:

- The palettes are samples from declared Munsell regions and do not validate twelve categories or personal suitability (`docs/palette-derivation.md:3-5`). Their axes are design intent, not measurements of people (`docs/palette-derivation.md:71-72`).
- Each season has 48 generated swatches (`docs/palette-derivation.md:111-115`), but the regions deliberately overlap (`docs/palette-derivation.md:94`). The prior diagnosis supplied with this commission measured 576 memberships but only 431 unique coordinates; Soft Autumn/True Autumn share 18 of 48 and Soft Summer/Bright Winter share 16 of 48.
- Resolver thresholds are chosen rules of thumb, with no labelled-person dataset (`docs/resolver.md:5,9-18`). The current nearest-swatch scorer produces low confidence in 31 of 36 fixtures and closely bunched scores (`docs/resolver.md:22-27`). The golden cases record implementation outputs, not ground truth (`docs/resolver.md:88-90`).
- The current picker has no representative iris data: all eye choices are brown and derive from clusters of one example iris, even though the source study contained 100 Korean eyes (`docs/input-swatches.md:76-84`). Its hair rows are individual participants rather than category means (`docs/input-swatches.md:28,41`).
- In the 640-combination input sweep, Deep Autumn receives 472 combinations and 485 results are low-confidence (`docs/input-swatches.md:109-129`). The commissioned brief reports that substituting better-separated references reduced Deep Autumn to 275 but raised low-confidence results to 523. Better coverage did not create discriminability.
- Project decisions already acknowledge that the best documented four-class automated accuracy is about .554, that no validation dataset is available, and that the current result must not be presented as a determination (`DECISIONS.md:14-23,99-101`; `docs/spec-corrections.md:47-53`).

These are tests of reproducibility and internal behaviour. They cannot answer whether the labels are natural categories or predict suitability.

## What could not be established

1. **Priority for the exact twelve labels.** Spillane and Sherlock (1995) is the earliest twelve-palette publication independently verified here, but some proprietary training systems and older books were not fully searchable. Establishing absolute first use would require library access to editions, trade training materials, and possibly archives or interviews. It would refine historical credit, not supply empirical validity.

2. **Any natural whole-person group count.** No source located combined calibrated skin, hair, and spatial iris measurements in a representative sample and performed unconstrained, replicated model selection for the number of groups.

3. **A soft/light biological or perceptual boundary.** No study located tests whether people form separate low-chroma and high-value groups or whether those labels predict different garment responses.

4. **Twelve-season predictive validity.** No study located assigns real participants to twelve seasons independently, then asks blinded third parties to judge controlled, reciprocal garment colours while controlling for general colour preference.

5. **Cross-population generality.** The useful studies are population-specific: Europeans across four cities, a five-identity US sample, Calabrians, Korean women or students, Thai observers, and predominantly White UK observers. None supports universal thresholds.

6. **Several older full methods.** Publisher access controls prevented full inspection of some practitioner books and journal articles. Abstract-only papers were retained only for the narrow claim their abstracts support and are marked accordingly in the ledger.

### What it would take

A decisive separability study would recruit a demographically broad, adequately powered sample; measure multiple skin sites, natural hair, and spatial iris zones under a specified illuminant, observer, instrument/profile, chromatic-adaptation method, and gamut; avoid predeclared season labels; compare continuous latent models with candidate mixture/cluster models; select group count with held-out criteria; and replicate it across site, device, and population.

A decisive predictive study would be separate and preregistered. It would assign participants without using outcome ratings, manipulate the same garment colours reciprocally beside the same calibrated faces, sample hue/value/chroma independently, use blinded raters other than the assigners, control global colour preferences and display conditions, compare twelve against simpler and continuous baselines, and treat a null or negative result as a valid endpoint.

Obtaining better input measurements is useful preparatory work, not a substitute for either study.

## Search method, bounds, and stopping rule

The search was conducted during one bounded session on 2026-09-12. It was breadth-first by design:

1. trace historical claims from current practitioner labels back through books and a contemporary thesis audit;
2. search quantitative skin, hair, and iris measurement literature for distribution or clustering results;
3. search perception, clothing, harmony, attractiveness, and personal-colour terms for independent suitability outcomes;
4. inspect recent computer-vision and recommendation papers to determine whether they introduce external ground truth or only learn inherited labels; and
5. follow the most relevant references and citing papers until the chains converged.

The search stopped when new queries were returning supervised systems built on the same practitioner labels, pigment studies without suitability outcomes, or citations back to the same small set of judgement experiments. Google Scholar and general search providers intermittently presented automated-traffic challenges; several publisher sites used access controls or Cloudflare. I used DOI records and primary publisher pages where possible, plus PubMed, PLOS/PMC, Nature/Springer, institutional repositories, Google Books, Internet Archive metadata/previews, Crossref/OpenAlex metadata, and the authors' public GitHub repository. Abstract-only use is disclosed below.

This was not a database-complete systematic review, and a bounded search cannot prove that no paper exists. The exact conclusion is: **within the stated breadth-first search, no empirical support for twelve natural whole-person categories or their predictive validity was located.** The consistency of that negative result across provenance, measurement, perception, and recent machine-learning literatures makes continued positive product claims unjustified without new evidence.

## Recommendation

Publish the negative answer plainly: **the twelve seasons are a traceable design convention, not twelve empirically established kinds of human colouring, and their predictive value has not been validated.** Keep the distinction between that conclusion and the narrower evidence that skin appearance can affect some garment-colour judgements.

For the current product, do not allow a better iris dataset, cleaner hair references, internally separated palettes, or a classifier's agreement with expert labels to stand in for the missing evidence. If twelve palettes remain, present them as designed comparison options. If the product wants to claim human types or predictive suitability, it needs the separability and outcome studies described above and must be willing to publish a negative result.

Send the Paparazzo data request as a coverage-and-measurement step, with colour-space metadata and reuse terms explicit. Its outcome can improve inputs; it cannot decide the number of seasons.
